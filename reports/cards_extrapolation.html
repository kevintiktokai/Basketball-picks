What the two-leg cards do to a bankroll

The engine builds at most one two-leg card a day, and both legs must win. Below are the two kinds of card, the same 15 seasons as the straight bets (2011-12 to 2025-26), and a projected next season. One unit is 1% of your bankroll, or $10 on $1,000.

2.5+ card

Two legs priced about 1.40–1.90, usually totals moved a few points in our favour, multiplied to 2.5 or more. Chosen for the highest chance that both win. This is the card you asked for at the start.

Main-line double

The day's two best straight picks at the posted totals, parlayed. Two −110 legs pay about 3.64.

Straight bets

For comparison: every single with a 55%+ chance, about 700 a season in 2021–26, staked one at a time.

If prices or the edge are worse

A parlay has to sit at one book, so you cannot take each leg's best price. The 2.5+ card's bought-down lines are priced by our model of the book (a 4.5% margin), not real quotes. These rows show one season from $1,000 when those things go against you.

Every season

Card rules were chosen on 2011–21; the bold seasons, 2021–26, are their first run with the rules locked. “$1,000 becomes” stakes 1/8 Kelly of the current bankroll on each 2.5+ card and starts each season fresh.

What these numbers assume

  • The 2021–26 seasons had already been used for the earlier straight-bet test, so the card results there are the locked rules' first run on seen seasons, not an untouched test. 2011–21 is where the card rules were chosen.
  • Card legs are priced at each game's best opening price across books. Placed at a single book at −110, the 2.5+ card earned per card and the main-line double in 2021–26.
  • About 9 in 10 of the 2.5+ card's legs are moved lines (alternate totals). Books price those differently and some don't offer them; check that the card's price is at or above what it shows.
  • The two legs are different games on the same day; same-day games were measured independent, so the chance both win is the product of the two.
  • Books cap parlay stakes and limit accounts that keep winning, so the compounding figures are an upper end.
